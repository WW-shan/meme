## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103)Abstract

### Background

In some survival studies, longitudinal biomarkers, along with baseline covariates, play crucial roles in predicting patient survival. Dynamic prediction models that incorporate updated longitudinal marker information offer updated survival predictions for patients. In this study, we employ a combination of the nonparametric gradient boosting machine learning algorithm and the landmark approach, which not only facilitates dynamic prediction but also circumvents the limitations of classical methods.

### Methods

We conducted two simulation studies under different scenarios to compare three dynamic prediction models: the joint model, the Cox landmarking model, and the Landmarking Gradient Boosting Model (LGBM). We compared the three dynamic survival prediction methods using AUC (Area Under the Curve) and Brier score metrics. Using the LGBM, we performed dynamic prediction at various landmark times on a real kidney transplant dataset in the presence of two longitudinal markers.

### Results

Simulation studies demonstrated that when there was a simple linear relationship between longitudinal markers and the survival process, the joint model outperformed both Cox landmarking and LGBM in terms of higher AUC (better discrimination) and lower Brier score (better overall performance) indices. Conversely, in scenarios characterized by complex and nonlinear relationships between longitudinal markers and the survival process, the LGBM outperformed the two classical methods, under conditions involving larger sample sizes (_n_ = 1000, 1500 vs. _n_ = 300, 650), higher censoring rates (90% vs. 30%, 50%), and later landmark times (3.5, 5, 6.5 vs. 0.5, 2). The application of LGBM to real kidney transplant data revealed that at early landmark time points, factors such as blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (variable importance [VIMP] = 0.34), age (VIMP = 0.26), creatinine (VIMP = 0.24), hypertension (VIMP = 0.10), and gender (VIMP = 0.06) were associated with the risk of kidney transplant failure. At subsequent landmark time points, creatinine, BUN, and age emerged as the most important factors associated with kidney allograft failure.

### Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that in situations where the relationships between variables are complex and the proportional hazards assumption does not hold, the LGBM method performs better than Cox landmarking and joint modeling for dynamic survival prediction in cases with large sample sizes, high censoring rates, and later landmark times.

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103)Background

Survival prediction models are used to predict the occurrence of a specific event in the future for prevention and decision-making in treating patients [[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR1), [2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR2)]. Survival models combine information on patient risk factors and use it to estimate the probability of experiencing a particular event [[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR3), [4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR4)]. Most traditional survival models, such as the Cox proportional hazards model, are based on variables such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, etc., which are collected from patients at baseline [[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR5)]. In contrast to baseline variables, which are measured at a single point in time, some variables change over time and are known as time-dependent variables.

Clinical biomarkers are often repeatedly measured during a patient’s follow-up to monitor their physical condition over time (such as through blood biomarkers in medical tests) [[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR6), [7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR7)]. These are time-dependent variables known as longitudinal biomarkers. Most conventional models overlook these longitudinal trends and instead use only baseline values, failing to capture the impact of biomarker changes on event risk.

Additionally, time-dependent Cox models cannot predict future survival probabilities, as they rely on biomarker values observed only at visit times and while the patient is alive [[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR5), [8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR8)].

When longitudinal markers are present, the prediction of patient survival should be dynamically updated as the values of these markers change. Dynamic prediction models can provide updated estimates by leveraging longitudinal marker information collected during patient follow-up [[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR9), [10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR10)]. Updated risk predictions by dynamic prediction models can help clinicians obtain a clearer view of disease dynamics so that they can make informed decisions at any time.

One of the common methods for the dynamic prediction of survival in the presence of longitudinal markers is the joint modeling method [[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR8), [11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR11)]. Joint models incorporate a sub-model for survival and a sub-model for longitudinal marker trends, linking these two components through a shared parameter [[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR11)]. Calculating conditional survival probabilities in joint models involves numerical integration, which is often computationally intensive [[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR12)]. A simpler approach for dynamic prediction in the presence of longitudinal markers is the landmarking approach. In the landmarking method, at each landmark time, a survival model is fitted to individuals who are at risk, utilizing patient information available up to that specific landmark time [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR13)].

Clinical studies often examine the relationship between independent variables and specific outcomes. Typically, linear relationships are used to examine these relationships because of their simplicity, but in some cases, the relationships between variables may be complex and nonlinear. For instance, in nephrology research, the relationship between hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels and mortality follows a nonlinear pattern, where both excessively high and low HbA1c levels are linked to increased mortality risk [[14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR14)]. It has also been shown that the relationship between BMI and various outcomes is a nonlinear U-shaped relationship [[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR15)]. Similarly, in breast cancer patients, the hazard of death or recurrence varies nonlinearly with age, higher in younger (< 40) and older (> 60) patients due to different biological and comorbidity profiles, while middle-aged patients (40–60) tend to have a lower hazard [[16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR16)]. Moreover, when multiple prognostic variables are involved, accurately determining their relationship with survival outcomes becomes increasingly complex. Additionally, when several longitudinal markers are considered, identifying relationships that align with model assumptions presents further challenges. In recent years, the advancement of new technologies and computer science has led to the development of machine learning algorithms such as artificial neural networks, support vector machines, decision trees, and random forests [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR13), [17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR17)]. Nonparametric machine learning algorithms are utilized as tools for clinical predictions without requiring assumptions about the data [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR18)]. Studies show that when relationships are complex or data is high-dimensional, machine learning models often outperform traditional prediction methods [[19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR19)–[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR23)].

Among machine learning algorithms, tree-based methods are particularly notable for their simplicity and useful interpretability. Methods such as bagging, random forest, gradient boosting, and Bayesian additive regression trees [[24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR24)–[26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR26)]. These methods are referred to as ensemble methods, as they combine many simple building block models (or weak learners) to create a highly powerful model [[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR17), [27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR27)].

The gradient boosting method is an ensemble nonparametric machine learning algorithm widely applied in various fields, including survival analysis [[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR28), [29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR29)]. Gradient boosting begins with a weak learner and incrementally improves its performance. In 2020, a study investigated dynamic survival prediction for binary data via a combination of the landmarking approach and a machine learning technique known as the super learner [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR13)]. Another study investigated dynamic survival prediction in the presence of longitudinal markers via a combination of the random forest algorithm with the landmarking approach [[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR5)]. The main limitation of the random forest algorithm is that the large number of trees can slow the prediction process [[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR17)]. The purpose of our study is to develop dynamic prediction models via a combination of the landmarking method and the gradient-boosting tree algorithm in the presence of two longitudinal markers to examine survival data by evaluating model performance through simulation studies and application to a real dataset. In this study, we use the advantages of nonparametric machine learning algorithms in addition to dynamic prediction by combining the landmarking dynamic prediction method with a gradient-boosting algorithm.

A gradient-boosting machine consists of two types of hyperparameters: boosting hyperparameters and tree hyperparameters. The boosting hyperparameters include the number of trees in the algorithm and the learning rate of each tree, which determines the contribution of each tree to the overall model. For dynamic prediction via the gradient boosting algorithm, a gradient boosting tree is employed at each landmark time point corresponding to the survival data. The gradient boosting algorithm offers an interpretation of the importance of each variable in the prediction. Variable importance can be presented in three ways:

#### Gain

Represents the average total reduction of the loss function for a given feature across all trees.

#### Coverage

Coverage reflects the number of instances (or data points) affected by a split or a particular decision rule in the model.

#### Frequency

The percentage represents the relative number of times a particular feature occurs in the trees of the model.

In our analysis, we used Gain as the measure of feature importance, as our primary goal was to understand which features contribute most to improving the model’s predictive performance. Gain reflects the relative contribution of each feature to the model by measuring the improvement in accuracy brought by a feature to the branches where it is used.

In dynamic prediction, the importance of variables may change at different landmark times, and these results can have vital clinical implications.

### Assessing the performance of the models

The performance of the joint model, Cox landmarking, and landmarking gradient boosting was assessed using two indices: the **AUC** and **Brier score**. The AUC determines the model’s discrimination ability, whereas the Brier score reflects the model’s overall performance (discrimination and calibration). Time-dependent versions of the AUC, AUC(s, w), and Brier score, BS(s, w), were used to account for censoring survival data. The AUC measures how well the model discriminates between subjects who have an event and those who are censored, whereas the Brier score is the mean square difference between the actual event occurrence indicator and the survival probability predicted by the model. The AUC ranges from 0.5 (no discrimination, such as random guessing) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination). The Brier score ranges from 0 to 1, with lower values indicating better overall performance. We used a linear regression model to analyze the response surface, where the response variable was the AUC or Brier score. The covariates included the dynamic prediction methods, sample sizes, censoring rates, landmark times, and correlations of longitudinal markers, all treated as factors. Interaction terms between the prediction methods and other factors were included to capture potential differential effects across scenarios.

### Application to real data

To demonstrate individualized dynamic prediction via the landmarking gradient-boosting method, we utilized retrospective cohort data from 731 kidney transplant patients who underwent transplantation between 2000 and 2015 at transplant centers in Mashhad city, Northeast Iran. Patients older than 65 years or those with more than one transplant were excluded from the study. Based on these inclusion criteria, 618 patients remained. The data included two longitudinal markers, serum creatinine and Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), which were recorded during the follow-up period after surgery. Due to the non-normal distribution of creatinine and BUN, logarithmic transformations were applied before analysis. Additionally, the baseline variables included patient age (years), gender (male/female), hypertensive status (hypertensive/non-hypertensive), serum creatinine within one month post-transplant (greater than 1.6 mg/dL or less than 1.6 mg/dL), time on dialysis pre-transplant (less than 24 months or more than 24 months), delivery type (living or cadaveric), and of immunosuppressant drugs, categorized as: Type 1 (cyc.cell.pred): a combination of cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone drugs; Type 2 (cyc.cell.pred.imo): a combination of cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, prednisone, and immunoglobulin. In our study, complete survival data were available for 558 individuals, representing approximately 90% of all participants. For the longitudinal biomarkers, missingness was minimal: 27 subjects (less than 5% of total participants) had missing values for BUN, and 1 subject (less than 1%) had missing values for creatinine. Given the low rate of missing data, complete case analysis was performed, consistent with guidelines suggesting that imputation offers minimal benefit when missingness is below 5% [[33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR33)]. In contrast, analyses tend to become biased when missingness exceeds 10% [[34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR34)]. Therefore, a complete case analysis was deemed appropriate for this dataset.

The objective was to employ the LGBM to dynamically predict kidney transplant survival. This model uses the exact time of kidney transplantation as the starting point and considers allograft failure, leading to a return to dialysis or, in some cases, death, as the event of interest. It is important to note that this definition refers to the clinical endpoint of graft failure, not specifically to immunologic rejection episodes. Patients lost to follow-up, those who survived until the end of the study, or those who died from unrelated causes were treated as right-censored. To predict survival probability, landmark times of 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, and 4.5 years, with a prediction window of 10 years (w = 10), were considered. Seven baseline covariates and two longitudinal markers were used for analysis (as listed in Fig.[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#Fig3)). Initially, the LGBM using imputed longitudinal markers via the Linear Mixed-Effects Model (LMM) was compared to the LGBM using the Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) method for longitudinal markers at each landmark time. Model performance was assessed using AUC and Brier scores, with 2-fold cross-validation averaged over 100 iterations.

To formally evaluate the functionality of the three methods in various scenarios, we conducted a response surface experiment for simulation studies 1 and 2. The results for simulation study 1 are presented in Table [A3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#MOESM1) in the Supplementary file. The reference scenario was defined as LGBM with a sample size of 1500, 90% censoring, and a landmark time at 6.5 years.

According to Table [A3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#MOESM1), the joint model showed a significant increase in AUC (β = 0.0690, _p_< 0.001) and a reduced Brier score (β = − 0.0197, _p_ = 0.0255) compared to the reference (LGBM). However, the joint model performed better under 30% censoring, as evidenced by improved Brier scores (interaction β = − 0.0145, _p_ = 0.0324), reinforcing its robustness in such scenarios. Earlier landmark times consistently worsened performance, increasing Brier scores (e.g., 0.5 years: β = 0.0413, _p_< 0.001) and reducing AUC (β = − 0.1712 and − 0.1304, _p_< 0.001), underscoring the difficulty of early prediction.

The formal response surface analysis for simulation study 2 is provided in Table [A4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#MOESM1) in the Supplementary file. The reference scenario was defined as LGBM with a sample size of 1500, 90% censoring, landmark time at 6.5, and covariate correlation of 0.8. In this analysis, both Cox landmarking (β = − 0.129, _p_< 1 × 10⁻⁷) and the joint model (β = − 0.147, _p_< 1 × 10⁻⁹) had significantly lower AUCs than LGBM, indicating inferior overall discrimination. However, the joint model demonstrated better calibration (Brier score) at early landmark times, particularly at time 0.5 (β = − 0.021, _p_ = 0.031).

A sample size of 300 significantly reduced AUC (β = − 0.077, _p_< 0.001) and increased Brier score (β = +0.028, _p_< 0.001), suggesting poorer performance.

These effects were partially offset by interaction effects with Cox (β = +0.110) and joint models (β = +0.067), both significant at _p_< 0.01. Lower censoring rates (30% and 50%), compared to 90%, led to significantly lower AUCs (β = − 0.097 and − 0.082; both _p_< 0.001) and higher Brier Scores (β = +0.115 and + 0.144; both _p_< 1 × 10⁻¹⁶), indicating greater prediction difficulty when more events occur.

These effects were mitigated by interaction terms, particularly for the joint model under 30% censoring (interaction β = +0.040, _p_ = 0.019). Earlier landmark times were associated with consistently lower AUC, with the strongest effect at landmark time 0.5 (β = − 0.119, _p_< 1 × 10⁻⁹). However, this reduction was alleviated by the joint model at times 0.5 and 2 (interaction β = +0.135 and + 0.147; both _p_< 0.001). Comparing scenarios with high (0.8) and low (0.2) correlation between the two longitudinal markers (Tables[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#Tab2) and [3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#Tab3); Supplementary Figs.2 and 3) showed a slight decline in model performance with lower correlation, though this difference was not statistically significant based on the response surface test (Table [A4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#MOESM1) in the Supplementary file).

These findings highlight that in complex scenarios, such as those explored in simulation study 2, where the proportional hazards assumption is violated and the relationship between the longitudinal marker and hazard is nonlinear, the LGBM model demonstrates superior predictive performance in conditions involving large sample sizes, high censoring rates, and later landmark times.

Supplementary Figure[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#MOESM1) (Supplementary file) presents a comparison of the survival probability plots from the three dynamic survival prediction methods—LGBM, Cox landmarking, and the joint Model—against the Kaplan-Meier estimator using simulated data under varying conditions: sample sizes of 1500 and 650, censoring rates of 30% and 90%, and landmark times of 0.5 and 6.5. At 30% censoring, both Cox landmarking and LGBM closely matched Kaplan–Meier estimates at 0.5 years. By 6.5 years, the joint model showed better alignment with Kaplan–Meier estimates across both sample sizes. At 90% censoring, Cox landmarking and LGBM consistently matched Kaplan-Meier well at both early and late landmark times. Generally, the difference in survival estimates arises because Kaplan-Meier provides unconditional survival from baseline, while model-based landmarking approaches adjust for covariates and estimate conditional survival from the landmark time, resulting in higher survival probabilities, particularly with lower censoring rates and later landmark times.

In terms of computational efficiency, the joint model incurred the highest runtime burden across all sample sizes. For instance, with 1500 samples, the joint model consumed approximately 2 min and 50 s of the 3-minute total runtime, accounting for over 90% of the computation time. This pattern was consistent across smaller datasets as well. When extrapolated to 500 iterations, the total runtime for the joint model ranged from approximately 375 to 1000 min, underscoring its high computational cost (see Table [A5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#MOESM1) in the Supplementary file). These runtime estimates were obtained on a system running Windows 10, with an Intel i7-4500U CPU (2.40 GHz) and 16 GB of RAM.

### Application to real data

According to the inclusion criteria and considering complete cases, a total of 558 kidney transplant recipients were enrolled in the study, of whom 40 (7.2%) experienced transplant failure during the follow-up period. The mean age of the patients was 34.26 years (standard deviation: 12.51), and 308 individuals (55.2%) were male. Patients were followed for up to 15 years, during which longitudinal biomarkers were recorded at multiple clinical visits. The median serum creatinine level was 1.2 mg/dL (interquartile range [IQR]: 1.0–1.5 mg/dL), and the median blood urea nitrogen (BUN) level was 31 mg/dL (IQR: 20–43 mg/dL).

A comparative analysis of the predictive performance of various modeling approaches—LMM LGBM, LOCF LGBM, LMM Cox Landmarking, LOCF Cox Landmarking, and the joint model—is presented in Fig.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#Fig1). According to Fig.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#Fig1), LMM LGBM provides the best discrimination (highest AUC) and the best calibration (lower Brier score) after the joint model for our real dataset. The cross-validated AUCs for the LMM LGBM at landmark times of 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, and 4.5 years were 0.76, 0.84, 0.80, 0.79, and 0.73, respectively. In comparison, the LOCF LGBM yielded AUCs of 0.74, 0.78, 0.81, 0.75, and 0.76, respectively. Both AUC and Brier scores confirmed that LMM-based imputation of longitudinal markers outperformed LOCF, and as such, all subsequent analyses on real data were conducted using the LMM LGBM model.

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103)Discussion

Dynamic prediction methodologies employ updated longitudinal data to predict patient survival time [[35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR35)]. Dynamic prediction can help in clinical decision-making based on patients’ conditions and lead to the selection of appropriate individualized treatment. As mentioned earlier, one of the common methods for dynamic survival prediction is the joint model. In this method, calculating the conditional survival probability for dynamic prediction requires the calculation of numerical integrals, which can be time-consuming [[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR8), [12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR12)]. Additionally, joint modeling requires assumptions about the relationship between the longitudinal marker trend and the survival and selection process of the longitudinal marker trend, the inaccuracy of which may lead to bias in the estimates [[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR8)]. Another classical approach is the Cox landmarking model, which also requires assumptions about the relationship between longitudinal markers and survival. However, this relationship is often unknown or difficult to specify correctly. In this study, we applied a gradient-boosted tree algorithm to landmark data. Gradient boosting is a nonparametric ensemble machine learning technique that learns from the data without requiring predefined functional forms. Nonparametric machine learning algorithm techniques can recognize and manage complex and nonlinear relationships rather than parametric machine learning algorithms and classic statistical methods [[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR18)]. Unlike parametric methods, gradient boosting trees construct an ensemble of decision trees, where each tree recursively partitions the feature space without assuming any specific functional form between input variables and the target. This tree-based structure inherently allows gradient boosting trees to: (1) Capture nonlinear effects: for example, if the relationship between a feature (e.g., age) and the target is piecewise or nonlinear (e.g., risk increases after a threshold), decision trees split the feature space at key points (e.g., age > 50), effectively modeling such nonlinearity. (2) Model variable interactions: Since decision trees split on multiple features along different paths, they naturally represent interactions. For instance, gradient boosting trees might first split on a variable like BMI and then, within that branch, split again based on age. This allows it to model conditions like “high risk only if both BMI > 30 kg/m 2 and age > 60 (years),” which traditional additive models may miss without explicit interaction terms.

Unlike classical dynamic prediction methods, nonparametric tree-based algorithms, such as gradient boosting, do not require the proportional hazards (PH) assumption [[36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR36)]. In contrast, classical methods like the Cox model rely on the PH assumption, and violations of this assumption can lead to biased estimates and misleading inferences [[37](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR37)]. Despite the advantages of machine learning algorithms, such as strong predictive performance and flexibility, they also present certain disadvantages. A key drawback is their lack of interpretability; for example, variable importance measures from models like LGBM do not offer clear or direct insights into the roles of individual predictors. In contrast, classical statistical methods such as the Cox proportional hazards model or joint models produce interpretable coefficients, allowing for a straightforward understanding of the direction and magnitude of covariate effects on the outcome. Also, ensemble algorithms such as gradient boosting are susceptible to overfitting, especially in high-dimensional or noisy data contexts. Therefore, careful hyperparameter tuning is crucial to balance the bias-variance trade-off, improve generalization, and ensure robust model performance [[38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR38)]. Among the three dynamic prediction frameworks assessed in this study—Cox landmarking, joint modeling, and LGBM—the joint model incurs the highest computational burden due to the need for numerical integration when estimating conditional survival probabilities. In contrast, landmark-based approaches (Cox and LGBM) avoid such intensive computations, enabling faster and more scalable dynamic prediction.

Accordingly, we explored the application of LGBM for dynamic survival prediction. As a machine learning algorithm, LGBM has the potential to outperform classical techniques under certain conditions.

In this study, we conducted various scenarios based on different sample sizes (_n_ = 300, 650, 1000, 1500), varying censoring rates (R% = 30, 50, 90), and distinct landmark times (LM = 0.5, 2, 3.5, 5, 6.5 years), using a prediction window of 10 years. These scenarios were designed to compare the predictive performance of the three methods under varying data complexities.

simulation study 1 demonstrated that, under a correctly specified linear association between the longitudinal marker and the hazard, the joint model outperformed both Cox landmarking and LGBM. These findings align with prior work by Rizopoulos et al., who showed that when the joint model assumptions are satisfied, it provides superior predictive accuracy compared to landmarking methods using either LMM or LOCF imputation strategies [[39](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR39)].

Simulation study 2 demonstrated that when there exists a complex, nonlinear relationship between longitudinal markers and the hazard function, and the proportional hazards (PH) assumption is violated, the LGBM model achieves superior predictive performance compared to both the Cox landmarking and joint models, under conditions of large sample sizes, high censoring rates, and later landmark times. The superiority of LGBM in these settings can be attributed to its nonparametric nature. Unlike traditional parametric models, LGBM can identify complex, nonlinear patterns in the data without requiring predefined assumptions about the functional form of the relationship between predictors and survival outcomes. This flexibility enables LGBM to handle model misspecification better and more accurately capture the underlying structure of the data [[23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR23)]. Moreover, both LGBM and the Cox landmarking method exhibit reduced sensitivity to violations of the PH assumption, partly due to resetting baseline hazards at each landmark time. Recent studies have compared landmarking machine learning algorithms with Cox landmarking and joint models in simulation studies. Pickett et al. [[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR5)] compared the random survival forest machine learning algorithm with two classical dynamic prediction methods: the joint model and Cox landmarking model. In line with our results, they demonstrated that when there is a nonlinear and complex relationship between longitudinal markers and the survival process, the random survival forest model outperforms the two classical dynamic survival prediction methods. Similarly, Tanner et al. [[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR13)] found that super learning, an ensemble machine learning algorithm, outperformed both the Cox landmarking and joint models in dynamic survival prediction for large sample sizes and high-dimensional data. In a study that compared joint modeling and landmarking, the bootstrap simulation results revealed that the joint model outperformed the other methods at early landmark times. However, at later landmark times, the Cox landmarking model demonstrated superior performance [[40](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR40)]. Our findings similarly support the superior performance of landmark-based methods over the joint model as landmark time increases. Based on these findings, in clinical practice, when a large number of baseline variables and several longitudinal markers associated with survival response are considered, their relationships with the longitudinal process are generally uncertain, and the proportional hazards assumption is not met. In addition, if the sample size is large or the censoring rate is high, the use of LGBM is recommended for dynamic prediction, especially for high landmark times.

In real data applications, we evaluated the predictive performance of the LGBM and conducted dynamic prediction using kidney transplant data. Our comparison between the LGBM performance with the LOCF and LMM imputation of longitudinal markers revealed that LMM imputation yields superior AUC and Brier scores compared with LOCF imputation. Consequently, dynamic prediction was executed using the LGBM with LMM imputation of longitudinal markers. The VIMP results from our study indicated that at early landmark times, both biomarkers—BUN and serum creatinine—along with baseline covariates such as age, blood pressure, and gender, are important predictors of kidney transplant survival. At subsequent landmark times, the most critical features for kidney transplant survival are serum creatinine, BUN, and age. The results from individualized dynamic survival prediction, in addition to offering an updated survival curve for each patient, underscore the significance of changes in the longitudinal markers, BUN, and serum creatinine, over time in estimating survival probability. Several studies have explored the impact of BUN on kidney transplant survival. Notably, a study by Alimi et al. [[41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR41)] investigated the combined effect of two longitudinal markers, BUN and creatinine, on the risk of allograft failure. These findings revealed a significant relationship between BUN levels and kidney transplant survival. Although the effect of creatinine was not found to be statistically significant in the study by Alimi et al., other studies, including ours, have demonstrated a significant association between creatinine levels and the risk of allograft failure [[42](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR42), [43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR43)]. Our results showed that the importance of serum creatinine increases with landmark time, whereas BUN decreases. The observed increasing importance of serum creatinine over time is consistent with its established role as a long-term marker of renal graft function and predictor of patient outcomes. Several studies have demonstrated that elevated creatinine levels, particularly in the late post-transplant period, are associated with chronic allograft dysfunction and increased risk of graft loss and mortality [[44](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR44), [45](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR45)]. In contrast, BUN, while reflective of short-term nitrogen balance and renal clearance, is more influenced by non-renal factors such as protein intake and catabolic state, which may diminish its long-term prognostic utility [[46](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR46)]. This likely explains the declining trend in the prognostic importance of BUN as the landmark time progresses.

Some studies have demonstrated that recipient age and the presence of hypertension are significantly associated with kidney transplant survival [[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR5), [41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR41), [47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR47), [48](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR48)]. Our study also identified age as a crucial factor in the risk of graft failure across all landmark times. According to our findings, hypertension emerged as an important feature only at the early landmark time of 0.5 years. However, at subsequent landmark times, hypertension was not deemed a significant factor. Other studies have also shown that hypertension increases the risk of transplant failure [[49](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR49), [50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR50)]. Research has shown that increased systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels after transplantation are associated with a higher grade of graft failure [[51](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR51)].

Our study had three limitations. First, in the simulation analysis of this study, owing to the numerous scenarios and the time-consuming nature of longitudinal marker substitution based on the LMM, the LOCF method was used, which may introduce bias. In this regard, a simulation analysis conducted to compare LMM and LOCF imputation in both Cox and LGBM landmarking models showed that LMM outperformed LOCF at lower censoring rates and later landmark times. Detailed results are provided in Table [A6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#MOESM1) (Supplementary file). Second, our study did not record other important longitudinal markers, such as hematocrit and Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR), which are important in evaluating kidney transplant survival [[52](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR52)]. In future studies, it is recommended to expand the variable set to include GFR and other key laboratory and clinical indicators, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of patient status and better capturing the multifactorial nature of graft survival risk. Third, there is a lack of information on competing risks to dynamically predict graft survival in kidney transplant patients considering competing outcomes [[53](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103#CR53)]. Future studies could explore the use of other machine learning algorithms (e.g., neural networks or bagging) on landmark data for dynamic survival prediction.

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513103)Conclusions

The gradient boosting tree algorithm, as a nonparametric machine learning method, can be applied to dynamic survival prediction in combination with the landmarking approach without requiring any assumptions about how longitudinal markers and the survival process are related, and the assumption of proportional hazards. Our findings demonstrated that in situations where the relationships between variables are complex and the proportional hazards assumption does not hold, the LGBM is an effective dynamic prediction method in cases with large sample sizes, high censoring rates, and high landmark times. Moreover, the model’s dynamic VIMP measures and individualized survival estimates offer clinically actionable insights. These tools can aid clinicians in making timely, personalized treatment decisions, potentially improving long-term patient outcomes.
