# Adapting Offline Reinforcement Learning with Online Delays

###### Abstract

Offline–to–online deployment of reinforcement learning (RL) agents often stumbles over two fundamental gaps: (1) the *sim-to-real gap*, where real-world systems exhibit latency and other physical imperfections not captured in simulation; and (2) the *interaction gap*, where policies trained purely offline face out-of-distribution (OOD) issues during online execution, as collecting new interaction data is costly or risky. As a result, agents must generalize from static, delay-free datasets to dynamic, delay-prone environments. Standard offline RL therefore learns from delay-free simulator logs yet must operate in delay-riddled reality, where the Markov assumption collapses and performance degrades. In this work, we introduce DT-CORL (Delay Transformer belief policy Constrained Offline RL), a novel offline RL framework explicitly designed to handle potential online delayed dynamics. DT-CORL (i) synthesises delay-robust actions utilizing a transformer based belief prediction function without ever seeing delayed observations during training, and (ii) slashes learning inefficiency relative to naive history augmentation approaches. Empirically, we validate DT-CORL on various benchmark tasks from the D4RL dataset under multiple delay configurations, demonstrating that our method outperforms both history augmentation approaches and vanilla belief-based baselines, bridging the sim-to-real latency gap while preserving the data efficiency.

## 1 Introduction

Real-world decision-making systems, including autonomous robots embodied assistants([jiao2024kinematics,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib13) ; [wang2023joint,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib44) ; [wang2023enforcing,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib45) ; [wei2017deep,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib46) ; [zhan2024state,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib54) ), always suffer from the issue of *observation and actuation delays*: sensory pipelines lag behind physical events, and control commands reach actuators only after non-negligible latency([cao2020using,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib5) ; [mahmood2018setting,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib25) ; [sun2022microfluid,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib35) ).
In this challenging setting, the Markovian property does not even hold, thus leading to significant performance degeneration.
To address this issue, existing delayed reinforcement learning (RL) methods usually reformulate this challenging problem as the augmented Markov decision process (MDP)([altman1992closed,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib1) ), a special form of partial-observable MDP (POMDP), since the delay makes the true state fully intractable.
Unfortunately, collecting *online* interaction data under delayed dynamics is expensive, risky, and often unsafe([li2023behavior,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib22) ; [yang2024case,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib52) ; [zou2015automatic,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib56) ).
At the same time, many entities already possess large *delay-free* trajectory data generated by high-fidelity simulators or legacy controllers([fu2020d4rl,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib8) ; [mu2021maniskill,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib26) ). These observations capture the task’s reward structure and nominal transitions but omit the latency found in deployment hardware. Consequently, we ask:

How can we train a robust policy *offline*—using only pre-collected, delay-free trajectories—so that it performs reliably when executed in a *delayed* real-world environment?

This question extends to two pressing needs: (i) leveraging static offline data without further interaction with the environment (offline RL), and (ii) robustly coping with latency at online deployment (delayed RL). Achieving both simultaneously promises safer development cycles, reduced sample complexity, and smoother sim-to-real transfer for latency-sensitive robotic and autonomous systems.

Delayed RL arises whenever perceptual or actuation delays disrupt the Markovian property, forcing agents to reason explicitly about the latency between true system states and available observations. Typical solutions involve either state augmentation[kim2023belief](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib17) ; [dida](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib23) ; [wu2024boosting](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib49) ; [wu2024variational](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib48) , which stacks multiple past history into an extended state space at the cost of significantly increased sample complexity, or belief-state approaches([chen2021delay,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib6) ; [karamzade2024reinforcement,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib14) ; [wu2025directly,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib47) ), which compress historical information into latent beliefs but suffer from compounding prediction errors as beliefs are propagated forward in time([wu2025directly,](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib47) ) and distribution mismatch when directly deployed.
Meanwhile, offline RL[levine2020offline](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib21)  seeks to derive effective policies purely from static, pre-collected datasets without additional online interaction. Its primary challenge lies in balancing policy conservatism, which avoids unsafe extrapolation, with exploration of actions potentially absent from the original data, leading to overly cautious or suboptimal policies. When delay handling meets offline training constraints, these challenges compound. Naive augmentation approaches create artificial, highly complex delay distributions rarely covered well by static offline datasets, further exacerbating sample inefficiency and dimensionality issues. Simultaneously, vanilla belief-state methods become vulnerable to unchecked drift, as offline data provides no corrective online feedback: at test time, the policy may query action–state pairs that are slightly outside the logged support; the frozen belief model must extrapolate there, propagating its error forward at every single step.

In this work, we propose DT-CORL (Delay-aware Transformer-belief Constrained Offline RL), a novel belief-based framework explicitly designed to tackle the compounded challenges arising in the offline delayed RL setting. Leveraging a transformer to predict compact belief states, DT-CORL converts the policy-constrained offline problem in a delayed MDP into an ordinary MDP optimization task. This end-to-end formulation both improves sample efficiency and contains model error during training, thereby sidestepping the distribution-mismatch and compounding-error issues that plague vanilla belief-state pipelines. Empirically, comprehensive experiments on the D4RL locomotion suite [fu2020d4rl](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib8)  across various delay scenarios—including varying delay lengths, deterministic delays, and stochastic delays—consistently confirm the superiority of DT-CORL over baseline methods, encompassing both augmented-state approaches and vanilla belief-based methods (i.e., direct integration of pretrained belief models with offline RL algorithms).

## 4 Our Approach

We now present the DT-CORL, a novel offline RL framework for adapting online delayed feedback. DT-CORL integrates transformer-based belief modeling with policy-constrained offline learning to address the challenges outlined in previous sections. This section describes how we construct delay-compensated belief representations, train a policy using belief prediction, and incorporate policy regularization to ensure effective deployment under delay. Specifically, we introduce a belief-based policy iteration framework that infers latent, delay-compensated states through a belief function, providing a compact and semantically grounded alternative. Then, we provide detailed algorithmic implementations. We further support this approach with a learning efficiency discussion to explain its efficiency benefits comparing with the augmented approach.

A seemingly simpler alternative is a *two–stage* pipeline:
(i) train a belief model offline;
(ii) freeze it and apply any delay-free offline-RL algorithm to the original delay-free samples, deploying the resulting policy with that fixed belief function.
This strategy, however, suffers from several drawbacks that DT–CORL avoids.
(i) Biased value targets.
In DT–CORL, the Bellman target in ([3](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#S4.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 4.1 Belief-based Policy Iteration ‣ 4 Our Approach ‣ Adapting Offline Reinforcement Learning with Online Delays")) is computed *through* the belief bΔ(⋅|x)b\_{\Delta}(\cdot|x)italic\_b start\_POSTSUBSCRIPT roman\_Δ end\_POSTSUBSCRIPT ( ⋅ | italic\_x ), so the critic learns on exactly the latent states the policy will later encounter.

(ii) Distribution mismatch.

(iii) Inferior sample efficiency in stochastic MDPs.
For stochastic dynamics, each offline tuple (s,a,r,s′)𝑠𝑎𝑟superscript𝑠′(s,a,r,s^{\prime})( italic\_s , italic\_a , italic\_r , italic\_s start\_POSTSUPERSCRIPT ′ end\_POSTSUPERSCRIPT ) reveals only a single next state, whereas DT–CORL reuses the same transition to generate ΔΔ\Deltaroman\_Δ latent states along the belief rollout, extracting richer supervision.

In short, embedding belief prediction *inside* the policy-iteration loop both contains model error and keeps the critic, policy, and belief model aligned—benefits a frozen two-stage pipeline cannot match, in addition to avoiding the dimensional explosion of raw state augmentation.

###### Remark 4.5.

Existing work has compared the sample-complexity of augmentation- and belief-based methods in the *online* delayed-RL setting [wu2024variational](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib48) ; [wu2024boosting](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib49) . The same intuition should carry over to offline learning, but a formal analysis would require assumptions—e.g., uniform data-coverage, linear value realisability, and bounded rewards—that are standard in offline-RL theory [bai2022pessimistic](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib3) ; [shi2022pessimistic](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib33) ; [xie2021bellman](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib51)  yet do not hold in our setting. Establishing tight offline bounds without these restrictive conditions is therefore an open problem that we leave for future work.

DT–CORL delivers markedly higher and more stable returns than both augmentation-based learners and vanilla belief-based approach in the deterministic-delay regime, validating the effectiveness of its belief-based behavior-constrained policy iteration.

## 6 Related Works

#### Delayed RL.

Delayed reinforcement learning arises in domains such as high-frequency trading [hasbrouck2013low](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib11)  and transportation [cao2020using](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib5) . While reward delay has been extensively analysed [han2022off](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib10) ; [arjona2019rudder](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib2) ; [zhang2023multi](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib55) , we focus on the more challenging *observation/action* delay. Existing methods fall into two categories: Augmentation-based, these algorithms concatenate the past ΔΔ\Deltaroman\_Δ actions (and sometimes states) to restore the Markov property, then optimise a policy in the enlarged state space. Notable examples include DIDA [dida](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib23) , DC/AC [bouteiller2020reinforcement](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib4) , ADRL and BPQL, which bootstrap large-delay learning with auxiliary small-delay tasks [wu2024boosting](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib49) ; [kim2023belief](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib17) , and VDPO, which frames delayed control as variational inference [wu2024variational](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib48) . A key drawback is that the augmented state dimension grows with ΔΔ\Deltaroman\_Δ, sharply increasing sample complexity. Belief-based, these approaches predict the latent current state and act in the original space. DATS adopts a Gaussian belief update [chen2021delay](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib6) ; D​-Dreamer leverages recurrent world models [karamzade2024reinforcement](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib14) ; D​-SAC blends delayed information via causal-transformer attention [liotet2021learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib24) ; and DFBT replaces the recurrent filter with a sequence-to-sequence transformer to reduce compounding error [wu2025directly](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib47) . Although more compact, belief rollouts still accumulate approximation error over time.
All of the above methods assume online interaction with a delayed environment. Aside from some imitation-style studies that pre-train on delay-free logs and then fine-tune online [dida](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib23) ; [chen2021delay](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib6) , we are unaware of any work that learns delay-robust policies *offline* using only delay-free data.

#### Offline RL and Online Adaptation.

Offline reinforcement learning aims to learn high-performing policies from fixed replay datasets without additional environment interaction, addressing safety and data-collection constraints common in robotics, healthcare, and recommendation systems [levine2020offline](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib21) . Early work focused on *value conservatism*, e.g., CQL [kumar2020conservative](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib20)  and IQL [kostrikov2021offline](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib18) , which lowers the Q-value of out-of-distribution (OOD) actions to prevent overestimation. Complementary *policy-constraint* methods such as BRAC [wu2019behavior](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib50)  and TD3 +BC [fujimoto2021minimalist](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib9)  regularise the learned policy toward the behaviour policy to stay within the support of the dataset.
Although purely offline training mitigates OOD failure, policies may remain sub-optimal if the dataset lacks trajectories near the optimum. A growing line of *offline-to-online* (a.k.a. hybrid) methods therefore initialise from an offline policy and continue fine-tuning with limited online interaction. Notable examples include AWAC [nair2020awac](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib28) , Conservative Fine-Tuning [nakamoto2023cal](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib29) , and Hy-Q [song2022hybrid](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib34) . In additions, there are also works trying to close the gap between the possible dynamic difference between simulation and real world scenarios[feng2023finetuning](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib7) ; [niu2022trust](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib30) ; [tiboni2023dropo](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00131v1#bib.bib39) . However, none of those works try to close the possible delay gaps between offline simulation/data and online real environments.

## 7 Conclusion

We introduced DT–CORL, the first offline–to–online delay adaption framework that learns delay-robust policies *solely* from delay-free trajectories.
By coupling a transformer–based belief predictor with a conservative, behaviour-regularised policy-iteration loop, DT–CORL eliminates the state-dimensional explosion of action-history augmentation while guarding against out-of-distribution actions that plague standard offline RL.
Extensive experiments on the D4RL locomotion benchmark confirm these gains: across deterministic and stochastic delays of up to 16 steps, DT–CORL consistently outperforms strong augmented-state and vanilla belief-state baselines, achieving much better learning efficiency.
However, in our setting, DT-CORL assumes a known delay length ΔΔ\Deltaroman\_Δ and deterministic actuation latency.
Extending the framework to *adaptive* or *heterogeneous* delays—where latency varies across tasks or even within an episode—remains open.
Moreover, while our transformer belief predictor copes well with the locomotion tasks studied here, scaling to vision-based manipulation may require integrating spatial attention or world-model priors.
Finally, an exciting direction is to combine DT-CORL with limited online fine-tuning, leveraging our conservative value estimates as an uncertainty-aware exploration bonus to further narrow the sim-to-real gap.
We hope DT–CORL and its accompanying analysis will spur broader exploration of delay-aware, data-efficient reinforcement learning.
